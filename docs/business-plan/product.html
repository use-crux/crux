Decide · product thesis

From deliberate turns to certified specialists

The product should evolve through three independently valuable horizons. Each horizon earns the evidence and users required for the next; none requires Crux to own model execution, GPUs, or customer weights.

Horizon 1: built foundationHorizon 2: candidate businessHorizon 3: conditional future
Product thesis: Crux is the independent evidence and proof layer for AI behavior—first for the harness around a model call, later for deciding whether that behavior belongs in context, routing, retrieval, or specialized weights.

Three horizons

1. Crux OSS — harness engineering

Developers deliberately compose prompts, context, memory, tools, routing, safety, and recovery around their chosen SDK. Crux explains and tests the resulting turn. This is the adoption engine and must remain useful without a cloud account.

2. Crux Reliability — evidence and release control

Teams turn production failures, feedback, variants, and evals into a governed evidence set. Crux compares changes, certifies acceptable behavior, and manages promotion across prompts, models, and policies. This is the nearest plausible paid product.

3. Crux Specialists — compile behavior into weights

Crux determines whether retrieval, examples, routing, SFT, preference tuning, or RFT is appropriate; delegates optimization; then certifies the candidate against the same independent bar. Build only after customer pull.

The commercial object: a Specialist

A Specialist is not merely a fine-tuned model. It is one bounded job and everything required to trust its execution.

Contract

One job

Inputs, outputs, tools, policies, acceptable uncertainty, latency, cost, and privacy requirements.

Evidence

One governed record

Demonstrations, preferences, rewards, eval cases, lineage, eligibility, splits, and missing coverage.

Proof

One release history

Candidate models, baselines, certification, canaries, fallback, drift, rollback, and retraining decisions.

What gets learned—and what does not

Put into weightsKeep in the harness
Stable task procedure and output formAuthorization, approvals, and access control
Company vocabulary and interaction styleMutable facts, inventory, policy versions, and customer records
Tool-selection patterns and bounded reasoning behaviorTool implementation, side-effect controls, retries, and recovery
Repeated customer preference patterns when explicitly eligibleSecrets, personal memory, retention boundaries, and deletion policy
Stable instructions currently repeated in every promptIndependent evals, safety checks, monitoring, and fallback

Crux earns trust by sometimes recommending no training. Interaction history is evidence, not automatically a dataset. An Eval is not automatically a safe RL reward: training repeatedly optimizes against weaknesses that are harmless in occasional certification.

The first paid product is smaller

Near-term product

Reliability Sprint + portable evidence package

For one production workflow, Crux captures the authored/runtime/Eval graph, reproduces failures, creates a representative Baseline, compares one cheaper or alternate model, and delivers a release recommendation. If specialization is justified, the package is ready for an external provider—without Crux running the job.

Why Crux can be different

Provider platforms

See optimization inputs

They receive training data, evaluators, and jobs. Their incentive is to retain training and inference traffic.

Crux

Sees product intent

It can connect source-authored definitions, turn-assembly decisions, runtime outcomes, and independent baselines before choosing a provider.

Product gate

Reliability before specialization

Do not expose a public “Train” or “Distill” product until Crux has: three active workflows, governed evidence export, held-out certification, and a customer who values the result above the provider’s built-in tooling.

Founder action

Prototype the artifact, not the trainer

Using one example workflow, manually assemble the future portable package: task contract, eligible demonstrations, failure slices, held-out evals, provider options, economics, and promotion criteria. Show it during interviews and ask which part teams would pay to avoid doing themselves.
