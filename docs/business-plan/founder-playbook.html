Execute · founder playbook

How to get the first five users

A design partnership is a bounded collaboration in which Crux solves one real problem while learning which parts should become product. This page turns that phrase into a sequence, scripts, boundaries, and a weekly routine.

Use nowNo paid tools requiredTemplates are starting points
The goal is not to convince everyone. Find a few teams whose current pain is expensive, whose workflow fits Crux, and who will invest access and attention in a measurable outcome.

Step 1 — find candidates

Build a spreadsheet with 25 companies. Look for public evidence rather than job titles alone:

  • AI is a visible part of the product, not an internal experiment.
  • Engineering posts mention evals, model migration, agents, prompt complexity, latency, or fine-tuning.
  • The product performs repeated classification, extraction, transformation, tool use, research, or review.
  • The team uses TypeScript, Vercel AI SDK, OpenAI/Anthropic SDKs, Convex, or compatible infrastructure.
  • A founder or engineer is reachable through GitHub, a community, an introduction, or a public email.
Record: company, workflow, visible pain, likely owner, evidence link, warm path, contact date, response, and learned language.

Step 2 — send a problem message

Cold / warm outreach template

Hi [name]—I noticed [specific product/workflow evidence]. I’m building Crux, an open-source toolkit that explains and tests everything around a model call: context, tools, routing, safety, and fallback.

I’m speaking with teams running [specific kind of workflow] to understand where production behavior becomes hard to reproduce or improve. I’m not trying to sell you a platform. Would you be open to a 20-minute conversation about how you currently debug and evaluate that workflow? I’m happy to share the patterns I’m finding.

Personalization means one true observation, not flattery. Follow up once after four to seven days with one useful artifact. Then stop.

Step 3 — run a 30-minute interview

MinutesQuestionWhat to learn
0–5“Walk me through the AI workflow from user input to outcome.”Real sequence, actors, tools, and boundary—not the pitch.
5–10“Tell me about the last time it failed in production.”Frequency, consequence, current debugging behavior.
10–15“How do you decide a prompt, model, or context change is safe?”Evals, reviewers, workarounds, release anxiety.
15–20“What production evidence do you retain? What can be reused?”Data quality, eligibility, volume, feedback, privacy.
20–25“What does this cost—in model spend, review, delays, or lost users?”Economic value and budget owner.
25–30“If we made one workflow explainable and protected, would you test it with me?”Commitment; ask for access or next step, not praise.

Do not demo until the workflow and last failure are understood. Ask permission to record; otherwise take notes. End by repeating the problem in their language.

Step 4 — qualify the design partner

Must have
  • One bounded production workflow.
  • Named technical owner and domain reviewer.
  • Representative cases or traces.
  • Measurable outcome and meaningful pain.
  • Weekly access for two to four weeks.
Nice to have
  • Permission for a named or anonymized case study.
  • Existing model alternatives to compare.
  • High traffic or meaningful review cost.
  • User corrections, feedback, or labels.
  • A clear path to paid continuation.

Step 5 — scope the sprint

Statement of work skeleton
  1. Workflow: one exact model or agent decision.
  2. Baseline: current quality, cost, latency, and known failures.
  3. Success: one primary metric plus non-regression constraints.
  4. Inputs: access, sanitized evidence, reviewers, and environment.
  5. Deliverables: instrumentation, cases, failure report, comparison, protected baseline, recommendation.
  6. Exclusions: application build, general MLOps, GPU hosting, labeling workforce, production SLA.
  7. Timeline: kickoff, evidence review, midpoint, result review.
  8. Commercial: fee, expenses, confidentiality, case-study rights, and continuation decision.

Free pilot rule

Offer free work only when the learning and public proof are worth more than cash. Require:

  • a strong ICP fit and real production evidence;
  • fast access and a committed reviewer;
  • permission to publish at least anonymized metrics and learnings;
  • a defined end date and no ongoing support promise;
  • an explicit paid continuation conversation.

After one or two free pilots, quote a price. A free customer who will not invest time is not a design partner.

Step 6 — close the learning loop

At the result meeting, show baseline → intervention → candidate → decision. Then ask four direct questions:

  1. What changed operationally because of this work?
  2. Which step was most valuable and which felt unnecessary?
  3. Would you pay to keep this evidence and release workflow running?
  4. Who else has this exact problem?

If successful, ask for a quote, case study, referral, and paid continuation while the result is concrete.

First-partner offer

One workflow, one baseline, one meaningful comparison

Keep the promise smaller than the Crux platform. A founder can personally deliver this, measure it, and turn it into the flagship case.

Services trap gate

Every engagement must compound

Decline or re-scope work that cannot produce at least two of: revenue, reusable product, public proof, qualified referrals, or decisive market invalidation.

Founder action

Send the first five messages

Do this before polishing another feature. Use real observations, record responses, and revise the workflow hypothesis after the conversations—not after inbox silence from one message.
